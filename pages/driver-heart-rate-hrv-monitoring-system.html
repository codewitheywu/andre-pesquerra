Driver Heart Rate & HRV Monitoring System

An embedded safety device that continuously monitors a driver's heart rate and heart rate variability while behind the wheel, triggering an audible alarm the moment readings drift into fatigue or distress territory. Built on an Arduino Nano with an optical pulse sensor.

Overview

🌱 The Premise
Fatigue is one of the quietest causes of road accidents — drivers rarely notice the moment they stop being alert. This project asked whether a low-cost embedded device could catch that moment from the driver's own body signals instead of waiting for the vehicle to drift out of its lane.

Heart rate variability was the signal of interest. HRV — the small timing differences between consecutive heartbeats — reflects how the autonomic nervous system is behaving, and it shifts measurably as alertness drops or as the body enters distress. Combined with raw BPM, it gives a far better picture of driver state than pulse alone.

🔧 The Build
The hardware stack was kept deliberately small so it could realistically live inside a vehicle:

• Arduino Nano — the controller, chosen for its footprint and low power draw. Small enough to mount discreetly, capable enough to run continuous sampling and analysis.
• Optical pulse sensor (MAX30102-class) — captures the photoplethysmogram signal used to derive both BPM and beat-to-beat intervals.
• Buzzer alarm — the output. Deliberately simple: when readings cross the danger threshold, the driver hears it immediately. No menus, no screen to look at, no reason to take eyes off the road.

💡 How It Works
The sensor streams raw optical data. The Nano filters that signal, detects peaks to isolate individual beats, and calculates the interval between them. From those intervals it derives BPM and the HRV metrics used for the fatigue and distress assessment.

The firmware runs a rolling evaluation rather than reacting to single readings — one odd beat is noise, a sustained trend is a warning. When the trend crosses threshold, the buzzer fires until the readings recover.

This was developed as a capstone-level research build, so the work went beyond making it function: it had to be defensible. Sampling rates, threshold values, and filtering choices all needed justification against the physiological literature, not just whatever number happened to work on the bench.

Challenges

🛠️ Technical Challenges
Biometric sensing inside a moving vehicle turned out to be a much harder environment than a desk:

• Motion artifacts — every bump, steering input, and hand adjustment corrupts an optical pulse reading. Raw sensor output in a moving car looks almost nothing like a clean lab waveform. Signal filtering and peak validation had to be aggressive enough to reject that noise without throwing away real beats.
• Sensor placement and contact pressure — optical sensors are brutally sensitive to how firmly and consistently they sit against the skin. Too loose and the signal vanishes, too tight and the reading skews. Getting a mounting approach that survived normal driving movement took repeated iteration.
• Ambient light interference — sunlight through a windshield leaks into the photodiode and washes out the signal. This needed both physical shielding and handling in software.
• Arduino Nano constraints — limited SRAM and no floating-point unit means you cannot just buffer long windows of samples and run expensive math on them. The filtering and HRV calculations had to be restructured to work within tight memory and clock budgets.
• False positives — an alarm that cries wolf gets ignored or unplugged, which makes the whole device useless. Tuning thresholds so genuine fatigue triggered the buzzer while ordinary variation did not was the hardest calibration problem in the project.
• Individual variation — resting heart rate and baseline HRV differ enormously between people. Fixed universal thresholds do not survive contact with real users, so the logic had to reference a baseline rather than an absolute number.
• Debugging without a display — with only a buzzer as output, every diagnostic pass ran through serial monitoring and logged sample data analyzed after the fact.

Outcomes

🏁 Outcomes
The system works as intended: it reads the driver's pulse continuously, derives HRV from beat-to-beat intervals, and sounds an alarm when the readings indicate fatigue or distress — with no interaction required from the driver.

What came out of it:

• A working, defensible prototype demonstrating that meaningful driver-state monitoring is achievable on hardware costing very little, not just on medical-grade equipment.
• Practical experience with biometric signal processing — filtering, peak detection, and interval analysis on noisy real-world data rather than clean sample sets.
• A real appreciation for embedded constraints. Writing code where memory and clock cycles are genuinely scarce forces decisions you never have to make on a web server.
• Hardware-side skills that carry over: sensor integration, wiring and mounting, iterative bench testing, and validating against physical conditions instead of assumptions.

It also sharpened the discipline of building for a hostile environment. A device that works perfectly on a workbench and fails in a moving car has not actually solved anything — the real requirement was surviving vibration, sunlight, and imperfect human contact all at once.
