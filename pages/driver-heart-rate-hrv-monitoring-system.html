Driver Heart Rate & HRV Monitoring System

An embedded safety device that continuously monitors a driver's heart rate and heart rate variability while behind the wheel, triggering an audible alarm the moment readings drift into fatigue or distress territory. Built on an Arduino Nano with an optical pulse sensor.

Overview

🌱 The Premise
Fatigue rarely announces itself before a driver drifts out of their lane. This project asked whether a low-cost embedded device could catch that moment from the driver's own body signals — using heart rate variability (HRV), the beat-to-beat timing shifts that reflect autonomic nervous system state, combined with raw BPM.

🔧 The Build
An Arduino Nano (small footprint, low power) runs the logic; an optical pulse sensor captures the photoplethysmogram signal; a buzzer is the entire output — no screen, no menu, nothing to take the driver's eyes off the road.

💡 How It Works
The Nano filters the raw signal, detects peaks to isolate individual beats, and derives BPM and HRV from the intervals between them. It runs a rolling evaluation rather than reacting to single readings — one odd beat is noise, a sustained trend is a warning — and fires the buzzer when that trend crosses threshold. As a capstone-level research build, every sampling rate, threshold, and filtering choice had to be justified against the physiological literature, not just bench-tuned.

Challenges

Biometric sensing in a moving vehicle is a much harder environment than a desk. Every bump and steering adjustment corrupts an optical reading, so filtering had to reject that noise without losing real beats. Sensor contact pressure is brutally sensitive — too loose and the signal vanishes, too tight and it skews — and sunlight through the windshield washes out the photodiode, needing both physical shielding and software handling. The Nano's limited SRAM and lack of a floating-point unit meant the filtering and HRV math had to fit tight memory and clock budgets. Tuning thresholds so genuine fatigue triggered the alarm without crying wolf — across people whose baseline HRV differs enormously — was the hardest calibration problem, and debugging without a display meant every pass ran through serial monitoring and logged data analyzed after the fact.

Outcomes

The system reads the driver's pulse continuously, derives HRV from beat-to-beat intervals, and sounds an alarm on fatigue or distress with no interaction required. It's a working, defensible prototype showing meaningful driver-state monitoring doesn't require medical-grade equipment, and it gave me real experience with biometric signal processing on noisy real-world data plus the discipline of embedded constraints — memory and clock cycles you can't hand-wave away. A device that works on a bench and fails in a moving car hasn't solved anything; the real requirement was surviving vibration, sunlight, and imperfect human contact all at once.
